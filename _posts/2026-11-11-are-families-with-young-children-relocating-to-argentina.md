If you are thinking about moving abroad with your children, you probably have a list of questions that goes well beyond visas. Will your children make friends? Can they attend a good school? Will you feel comfortable taking them to the park? And, perhaps most importantly, are other families making this move, too?

In Argentina, **thousands of young children are receiving legal residency each year**. We know this because we recently obtained detailed figures directly from Argentina's **Dirección Nacional de Migraciones (DNM)**, the national immigration authority, through a formal access-to-public-information request. The data covers 2024, 2025 and January through September 2026. We analyzed it ourselves for Life in Argentina.

Between January 2024 and September 2026, Argentina recorded **36,331 temporary and permanent residency grants for children aged 0–14**. Of those grants, **18,407 involved children aged 0–7**. This is a significant number of young people becoming part of the country's resident population, or formalizing a residence they already had.

It is not, however, a count of 36,331 families moving to Argentina. Government records identify residency grants by age, not entire households, and a grant does not necessarily mean that someone arrived from abroad that year. That distinction matters. The figures give us reliable evidence of immigration activity involving children, while the experience of raising a family here tells another, more personal part of the story.

### Are families with young children really relocating to Argentina?

**Yes. Children are an established part of Argentina's immigration activity, although the available data cannot tell us how many complete family households relocated.** Argentina grants residence to thousands of children in the youngest age groups, including babies, preschoolers and primary-school-aged children. Those grants reflect a country in which immigration is not limited to solo travelers, retirees or young professionals.

We think this is an important part of Argentina's story. People often discover Buenos Aires through its restaurants, architecture, nightlife or remote-work possibilities. Far less attention is given to parents who want to build an everyday life here: taking a child to school, getting to know the families in their neighborhood, and finding a local community.

Our own experience as Buenos Aires-based immigration lawyers is that families do not usually make relocation decisions based on a single advantage. They look at the whole picture: immigration rules, language, education, healthcare, housing, the kind of childhood they want to offer and whether a place feels like somewhere they could belong.

### What does the newest official immigration data show about children?

**Our original analysis of the DNM records found 36,331 temporary and permanent residency grants for children aged 0–14 between January 2024 and September 2026.** Temporary residency grants to this age group rose by approximately 16% from 2024 to 2025.

| Period | Temporary residency grants | Permanent residency grants | Total grants, ages 0–14 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2024 | 7,120 | 8,295 | 15,415 |
| 2025 | 8,258 | 5,478 | 13,736 |
| January–September 2026 | 5,540 | 1,640 | 7,180 |
| **Total** | **20,918** | **15,413** | **36,331** |

*Source: Dirección Nacional de Migraciones. Data obtained directly by Life in Argentina through an access-to-public-information request in 2026; calculations by Life in Argentina. The figures cover Argentina nationally, not Buenos Aires alone. The 2026 data is partial-year.*

The youngest children make these figures particularly interesting. In 2024, **3,772 temporary residencies** were granted to children aged 0–7. In 2025, the figure increased to **4,508**, a rise of approximately **19.5%**. Another **3,059** temporary residencies were granted to children in that age group during the first nine months of 2026.

These are administrative grants, not unique family units or counts of new arrivals. Some children may already have been living in Argentina, and the dataset does not establish which adults live with them. The government also uses an age band of 15–21, so we cannot isolate teenagers aged 15–17 and have not included that group in our totals. The comparison between complete years 2024 and 2025 is more meaningful than comparing either year with the incomplete 2026 period.

Why do we think this information matters? Because it gives families something more useful than an anecdote. We requested these records ourselves so we could share **recent primary-source information about the immigration system** with people considering a move. The figures are current through September 2026, the latest month included in the government's response to us, rather than estimates drawn from older articles.

### Why might parents choose Argentina to raise their children?

**Argentina offers a combination of family-centered social life, educational options, urban amenities and opportunities to spend time together.** For parents considering a move, those qualities may be just as important as financial calculations.

Raising children can feel increasingly complicated in many countries. Parents may struggle with expensive childcare, long commutes, limited free time or a sense that family activities always require elaborate planning. Moving abroad will not make every one of those problems disappear, and Argentina has its own economic challenges. Yet it can open the door to a different rhythm of life.

There is a strong tradition of multigenerational gatherings, long family meals, neighborhood clubs and time spent in parks and public squares. Children are often included in social occasions, whether that means a Sunday lunch with relatives, an evening at a casual restaurant or a birthday celebration with several generations around the table.

For families bringing income or savings from abroad, some expenses may compare favorably with those in their home countries, although the outcome depends heavily on exchange rates, housing, school fees and lifestyle. We encourage parents to make a realistic household budget instead of assuming that everything in Argentina will be inexpensive.

### What is everyday family life like in Buenos Aires?

**Much of family life in Buenos Aires happens in its neighborhoods: at the plaza, the playground, the local café, the school gate and the sports club.** These ordinary places can be where an unfamiliar city gradually becomes home.

In Buenos Aires, you will often see parents and grandparents with children in neighborhood squares, especially after school and on weekends. A plaza is not simply somewhere to let a child run around. It can become part of the family's routine and a place to recognize the same faces every week. In our experience, that makes it easier for newcomers to begin building friendships without feeling that every social interaction needs to be organized in advance.

The city government reports **417 public playgrounds** across its parks, plazas and smaller squares. Some have themed play structures, and places such as Parque Centenario, Parque Chacabuco and Plaza Armenia offer examples of how children's activities are incorporated into public space. You can explore the city's [official guide to public playgrounds](https://buenosaires.gob.ar/gcaba_historico/patios-de-juegos-de-la-ciudad).

Buenos Aires is also a large city, so neighborhoods differ in traffic, noise, housing, access to parks and transportation. For a family with a stroller or a toddler, the practical details of a particular block may matter more than a neighborhood's general reputation. Visiting the areas around potential homes and schools, ideally at different times of day, is time well spent.

### Is Buenos Aires a safe city for families with children?

**Buenos Aires has comparatively low levels of lethal violence, according to recent official statistics, but it is not a crime-free city.** As in any major urban area, choosing a neighborhood and taking everyday precautions remain important.

Argentina's [National Criminal Information System](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/seguridad/estadisticascriminales) reported an intentional homicide rate of **3.6 per 100,000 inhabitants in 2025**. In Buenos Aires City, the government's [2025 crime report](https://cdn.buenosaires.gob.ar/datosabiertos/datasets/ministerio-de-justicia-y-seguridad/delitos/2025_informe_ejecutivo.pdf) recorded **2.53 intentional homicides per 100,000 inhabitants**, the lowest rate in the city's official historical series beginning in 1995.

These numbers are meaningful, but homicide rates do not measure every aspect of a family's safety. Petty theft, robbery, traffic conditions and differences between neighborhoods deserve attention too. Parents should look at both official statistics and the practical conditions around a potential home, school or daily commute.

One reason we enjoy living in Buenos Aires is that many neighborhoods make it possible to organize daily life around nearby services, cafés, parks and schools. That can be especially appealing when you have young children and want to spend less time traveling across a city. It is an advantage to explore, rather than a guarantee of the same experience everywhere.

### Will children from other countries feel welcome in Argentina?

**Argentina has a long history of immigration and a multicultural social life, making it possible for children to encounter different languages and backgrounds as they grow up.** A child arriving from abroad will still need time and support to adjust.

Buenos Aires is home to people with roots across South America, Europe, Asia and elsewhere. Argentina's residency records include applicants from a wide range of nationalities, although the data supplied to us does not cross-tabulate nationality and age, so we cannot reliably state which countries the youngest children come from.

For expat parents, language is often one of the biggest worries. A young child may begin learning Spanish through play, school and daily contact, but that process varies enormously from one child to another. Some adapt quickly; others need reassurance, time and perhaps additional language support. Children can also benefit from maintaining their home language and their connections to friends and family abroad.

What we particularly appreciate about Argentine culture is how visible children are in everyday social life. Families commonly bring children to restaurants, cafés and gatherings. That warmth does not eliminate the hard moments of adjusting to a new country, but it can make those moments less isolating.

### Can foreign children attend school in Argentina?

**Yes. Foreign children have a legal right to be admitted to preschool, primary and secondary education in Argentina, including when their immigration status is irregular.** Article 7 of [Immigration Law No. 25,871](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/92016/actualizacion), as amended in 2025, expressly protects admission to public and private institutions at these levels and prohibits discrimination based on irregular immigration status.

That right does not guarantee a seat at a particular school. Parents still need to consider registration deadlines, available places, the child's age, language needs and the documents requested during enrollment. Depending on the family's circumstances, Buenos Aires offers public schools, private schools and bilingual or international programs.

For parents of young children, it is worth asking what the child's actual school day would look like, not just comparing academic programs. How far is the school from home? Is the schedule compatible with your work? What language support is available? Will your child have the chance to form friendships close to home? Those questions can make the transition easier.

### What healthcare options should relocating parents consider?

**Families should arrange dependable medical coverage and understand which public or private services they can access under their immigration status.** Buenos Aires has pediatric services and a broad range of public and private healthcare providers, but the legal rules for foreigners have changed.

Under Article 8 of [Immigration Law No. 25,871](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/92016/actualizacion), emergency medical care may not be denied on the basis of immigration status. Permanent residents have access to public healthcare on the same terms as Argentine citizens. For people without permanent residence, routine care in **nationally administered** healthcare facilities may require insurance or advance payment, as implemented through [Ministry of Health Resolution 1066/2026](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/resolucion-1066-2026-428778/texto). Rules and arrangements at facilities run by other jurisdictions can differ.

If you are relocating with a baby or a young child, discuss pediatric appointments, vaccinations, medications and any ongoing medical needs before you travel. A clear healthcare plan is reassuring in a new country, particularly during the first months of settling in.

### How can parents apply for Argentine residency with their children?

**Parents and children need immigration arrangements appropriate to their individual circumstances; a parent's eligibility does not automatically resolve every child's application.** Argentina recognizes temporary, permanent and transitory residence categories. Which one applies depends on nationality, family relationships, work, studies, qualifying income and other legal criteria.

Families may need to prepare valid passports or accepted travel documents, birth certificates establishing the relationship between parent and child, and any required parental consent or custody documentation. Foreign documents may need apostilles or other legalization and certified Spanish translations. The exact requirements depend on the application route and where it is filed.

This becomes particularly important when only one parent is traveling, when parents have different immigration statuses, or when the child has a legal guardian. We recommend reviewing the entire family's situation **before** arranging translations, paying fees or making irreversible travel plans.

Argentina's immigration legislation has changed since 2025, so online advice written several years ago may be out of date. At Life in Argentina, our work as immigration lawyers involves checking the legal framework against the actual circumstances of the people we assist, rather than treating every family as though it fits the same checklist.

**Argentina can be a wonderful place to raise children, especially for parents who value community, cultural life and opportunities to spend time together.** Whether it is the right choice for your particular family depends on practical matters as well as personal priorities.

Some families are looking for a different cost of living. Others want their children to become bilingual, experience a new culture or grow up in a place where going to the park and seeing friends can be part of ordinary life. Others simply want to reconsider what a good childhood and a manageable family routine might look like.

We are lawyers, but we are also people who live in Buenos Aires and genuinely love this city. One of the things we love most is that children are part of its everyday life. You see them in the plazas, at neighborhood restaurants and at long family lunches. There is room here for the little rituals that make a place feel like home.

No country is perfect, and moving with children requires thought and preparation. But if you are looking for a welcoming, culturally rich place to build a family life, Argentina deserves to be on your list.

### Frequently asked questions about relocating to Argentina with children

#### Do children need their own Argentine residency applications?

A child needs their own lawful immigration status and supporting documentation. The procedure may be coordinated with a parent's application, but a parent's residence approval does not by itself establish every detail of the child's eligibility.

#### Can my child enroll in school before receiving an Argentine DNI?

Argentina's immigration law protects access to preschool, primary and secondary education even when a child's immigration status is irregular. Schools may ask for available identity and educational records as part of enrollment, but lack of an Argentine DNI should not by itself be treated as a lawful reason to deny the educational right.

#### Are American families moving to Argentina with children?

U.S. nationals appear in Argentina's residency statistics, but the data provided to Life in Argentina does not cross-tabulate nationality with children's ages or identify complete households. We cannot use it to give a reliable count of American families moving with children.

#### Does the new data show that more families moved in 2026 than in 2025?

No. The 2026 dataset covers only January through September, and residency grants are not the same as household relocations. The clear year-on-year increase we can establish is in **temporary residency grants for children aged 0–14 from 2024 to 2025**, which rose by approximately 16%.
