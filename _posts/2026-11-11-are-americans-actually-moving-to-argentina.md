**Yes, Americans are taking concrete steps to establish legal residency in Argentina.** In the first nine months of 2026, Argentina granted **1,280 temporary residencies to U.S. citizens**, according to newly obtained government records. That is already more than the **1,254 temporary residencies granted during all of 2025**.

If you have been wondering whether Americans are really making Argentina their home, that is a meaningful sign. These are not travel searches or social media posts about wanting to leave the United States. They are immigration decisions issued by Argentina's national immigration authority. But they are not a head count of Americans who packed their bags and moved here, either. A residency approval can involve someone already in the country, and the same person may receive different approvals over time.

We wanted to understand the difference. So, at **Life in Argentina**, our Buenos Aires-based immigration law firm, we made a formal public information request to the **Dirección Nacional de Migraciones (DNM)**, Argentina's National Immigration Directorate. The agency gave us detailed residency records covering 2024, 2025 and part of 2026. Our legal team analyzed the response to see what it actually tells us about Americans establishing residency here.

**This is original reporting based on government information we requested ourselves**, not a figure repeated from another expat website. And because we help people navigate Argentina's residency system every day, we can explain both the numbers and the legal decisions behind them.

### Are Americans really getting residency in Argentina?

**Yes. The latest available DNM records show substantial numbers of residency grants to U.S. citizens in each of the last three years.** The clearest sign of current interest is in *residencia temporaria*, or temporary residency, a legal category commonly used by people who want to live in Argentina for a defined period rather than merely visit as tourists.

Here is what the government reported for the nationality recorded as *estadounidense* (U.S. citizen):

| Type of residency granted | 2024 (full year) | 2025 (full year) | 2026 (Jan–Sep) |
|:--|--:|--:|--:|
| Temporary residency | 1,378 | 1,254 | **1,280** |
| Permanent residency | 358 | 296 | 95 |
| Transitory residency | 316 | 341 | 296 |
| **Total residency grants** | **2,052** | **1,891** | **1,671** |

*Source: Dirección Nacional de Migraciones, official response to a public information request submitted by Life in Argentina, administrative file **EXP-2026-99227630**. Analysis published November 11, 2026. The 2026 data covers January through September only; October through December are not included.*

These categories describe different legal permissions. **Temporary residency** is generally the category most relevant to someone planning to live here on an eligible work, study, family, pension or income basis. **Permanent residency** is a different status, available to applicants who meet its legal requirements. **Transitory residency** concerns specified short-term purposes; these records are not a count of everyone who entered Argentina as a tourist. You can find the legal framework in [Argentina's Migration Law No. 25,871](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/92016/actualizacion).

The table does not tell us how many Americans now live in Argentina. It does establish something more modest and more reliable: Americans are obtaining formal Argentine residency, and temporary residency grants have been particularly active in 2026.

### Are more Americans applying to live in Argentina in 2026?

**The records show more temporary residency grants, but they do not show how many applications were filed.** Argentina granted 1,378 temporary residencies to U.S. citizens in 2024 and 1,254 in 2025. By September 30, 2026, the figure had reached 1,280, **26 more than the entire previous year**. That is a 2.1% increase over the full-year 2025 count, with three months of 2026 still outside the dataset.

There is another way to look at it. The government averaged about **105 temporary residency grants to Americans per month in 2025**, compared with about **142 per month during January to September 2026**. This is a comparison of approvals, not a forecast of how many people will arrive before December. Administrative processing and the timing of decisions can affect monthly averages.

Americans also represented a larger share of all temporary residency grants in the available 2026 records: **2.85%**, compared with **1.75% in 2025** and **2.02% in 2024**. During January to September 2026, the United States ranked **eighth among nationalities** in this category and was the highest-ranked country outside South America.

We would not describe this as proof of a mass American exodus. The U.S. figures remain much smaller than those for several neighboring South American countries, and the permanent residency figures tell a different story. But the temporary residency numbers give us a real, documented reason to say that Americans are taking Argentina seriously as a place to live.

### Does getting residency mean someone actually moved to Argentina?

**Not necessarily, and that distinction is essential.** Residency grants are official administrative decisions. They are not the same as new arrivals at the airport, new households established in Buenos Aires or the number of people still living in Argentina today.

Someone might receive temporary residency after spending time here under a different immigration status. Another person might obtain permission to live here and later decide to return to the United States. Permanent residency approvals may relate to people who have lived in Argentina for years, rather than people who arrived in 2026. The records also count **grants**, not necessarily unique individuals across the three years.

For the same reason, we cannot use this dataset to say that American retirees, remote workers or young families are behind the change. Migraciones supplied separate breakdowns by nationality and by immigration category or grounds, but not the combined U.S.-citizen breakdown necessary to make those claims. Nor does the dataset identify which cities the recipients chose.

That might sound less exciting than a headline announcing that thousands of Americans are flooding into Argentina. We think it is more useful. If you are considering a move, you deserve information you can actually rely on.

### Why would an American consider living in Argentina?

**For many people, the attraction is the possibility of a different kind of everyday life.** Not necessarily a dramatic reinvention, and not simply a cheaper version of the United States. It can be as ordinary as having more opportunities to walk places, spending an unhurried Sunday with friends or living in a city where cafés, parks, bookstores and neighborhood restaurants are part of the daily routine.

We live and work in **Buenos Aires**, and that is a large part of why we care about helping people settle here. It's a city with extraordinary theaters, music, food and architecture, but it is also a place of everyday rituals: stopping for coffee, meeting friends late for dinner, taking a walk through a leafy neighborhood or spending an afternoon in a park. There are people from many backgrounds, and the city has a long history of immigration. You do not have to be born here to feel curious about belonging here.

And Argentina extends well beyond Buenos Aires. Some people prefer Mendoza's wine country and proximity to the mountains. Others look to Córdoba, Patagonia or smaller cities for a different pace of life. What works for a single professional may be quite different from what works for a family or someone planning retirement.

Of course, a relocation is not a vacation. Learning Spanish makes many parts of daily life easier. Rental agreements, financial transfers, taxes and healthcare arrangements take preparation. Prices and exchange rates change, and safety varies by neighborhood and circumstances, as it does elsewhere. **We love Argentina without pretending that moving here eliminates every problem.** The goal is to find out whether the life you can realistically build here is the one you want.

### Can an American move to Argentina legally in 2026?

**Yes, if they qualify under an Argentine immigration category.** Having a U.S. passport does not automatically give someone the right to live in Argentina indefinitely. For most Americans considering a longer stay, the practical starting point is identifying an appropriate residency basis and gathering evidence to support it.

Depending on your circumstances, possible routes include:

- **Pensionado residency**, for people receiving a qualifying regular foreign pension or retirement benefit.
- **Rentista residency**, for qualifying income from foreign assets, with specific documentation and financial requirements. Some situations involving foreign savings require a more individualized legal assessment.
- **Employment-based residency**, where the job and employer meet Argentine immigration requirements.
- **Student residency**, for study programs that meet the applicable rules.
- **Family reunification**, for people with qualifying family ties to Argentine citizens or residents.

The DNM publishes official guidance for [rentista](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/servicio/obtener-una-residencia-temporaria-como-rentista), [pensionado](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/node/152616) and [family-reunification](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/servicio/obtener-una-residencia-temporaria-por-reunificacion-familiar) residency, among other categories. Requirements depend on the route and can change. In particular, rules amended in 2025 affect aspects of the current residency framework, which is one reason it is risky to rely on old blog posts or advice from a friend who applied years ago.

If you want a practical overview, read our [guide to temporary residency in Argentina](https://lifeinargentina.com/blog/how-can-i-get-temporary-residency-in-argentina/) or our article on [retiring in Argentina with savings and investments](https://lifeinargentina.com/blog/can-i-retire-in-argentina-with-savings-and-investments/).

### Do Americans need to arrange residency before arriving in Argentina?

**Not always.** U.S. passport holders who enter under Argentina's ordinary visa-exempt tourist rules may generally be able to request a change to a qualifying residency category while in Argentina. But the ability to apply from inside the country and eligibility for residency are two different questions. Being admitted as a tourist does not, by itself, qualify you to become a resident.

For many applications, the process involves **RaDEX**, Migraciones' online residency platform, followed by document review and any appointments required by the authority. A valid passport, criminal-record documents where applicable, proof of an Argentine address and evidence of the chosen legal category may be required. Foreign documents often need an apostille or other legalization and appropriate Spanish translations.

You can read more in our article, [Can I switch from tourist status to residency without leaving Argentina?](https://lifeinargentina.com/blog/apply-for-argentina-residency-as-a-tourist-without-leaving/). Before you book a one-way flight or pay for a long-term lease, we recommend reviewing the immigration route you can actually document, as well as your separate tax and financial arrangements.

### How did Life in Argentina get immigration figures that most relocation guides don't have?

**We asked the Argentine government for them directly.** Rather than basing this article on social media conversations, tourism statistics or generalized claims that more Americans are moving abroad, we submitted a **formal access-to-public-information request** to the Dirección Nacional de Migraciones under Argentina's [Public Information Access Law No. 27,275](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/normativa/nacional/265949/actualizacion).

We requested figures on the residencies Argentina granted in 2024, 2025 and 2026, including their legal categories and the nationality, age, gender and immigration-ground breakdowns recorded by the agency. The DNM responded in administrative file **EXP-2026-99227630**, providing the official records we used for this article. We then consolidated the tables, checked the totals and separated what the data supports from what it does not.

That matters because immigration advice is only as useful as the information behind it. A website may explain how a residence category works in theory. **We also want to understand what Migraciones is doing in practice**, which trends appear in its records and where the evidence stops. Accessing primary government information, reading the current legal rules and working with clients in Buenos Aires are different parts of the same job.

Our figures are not a private government database or a claim that no one else can request public information. They are **newly obtained, first-hand government data that our firm took the initiative to request and analyze**. For readers comparing where to get immigration advice, that is the difference we want to make clear: at Life in Argentina, we do more than repeat the usual relocation checklist.

*Methodology: Figures are taken from the DNM response's nationality-by-residency-category tables. We did not add separate age, month, gender or immigration-ground tables to the nationality totals, because they describe the same underlying grants from different perspectives. The response gives U.S.-nationality counts by year and residence category, but not a monthly U.S.-specific series or a U.S.-specific cross-tabulation by age or immigration ground. Data for 2024 and 2025 is full-year; 2026 covers January 1 through September 30. Figures describe residency grants, not applications, border crossings, first-time movers, unique people across years or the current American resident population.*

### Frequently asked questions about Americans living in Argentina

#### Are more Americans moving to Argentina in 2026?

**We cannot measure new movers from the records obtained, but we can document an increase in temporary residency grants.** Argentina granted 1,280 temporary residencies to U.S. citizens from January through September 2026, compared with 1,254 during all of 2025. That is evidence of residency activity, not a count of relocations.

#### How many Americans currently live in Argentina?

The DNM figures used here do not answer that. They report residency decisions issued over time, not how many U.S. citizens were living in Argentina on a particular date. A resident-population estimate requires a different dataset.

#### Are most Americans moving to Buenos Aires?

We cannot tell from this data. The nationality tables do not identify the destination city or province. Buenos Aires is where our firm is based and where we have firsthand relocation experience, not a conclusion drawn from these figures.

#### Are American retirees or digital nomads behind the increase?

The available records do not establish that. We have category totals and U.S.-nationality totals, but not a combined breakdown showing how many U.S. citizens qualified through pensions, investments, work, study or other grounds. Nor do the data report the reasons people chose Argentina.

#### Can a U.S. citizen live in Argentina without an Argentine employer?

Potentially, yes. An eligible American may qualify through a foreign pension, qualifying assets or income, recognized studies, family ties or another lawful category. There is no universal residency granted simply because someone has a U.S. passport or sufficient money in a bank account.

#### Can Americans get permanent residency in Argentina?

Yes, when they meet the applicable legal requirements. A temporary resident may become eligible to apply for permanent residence after meeting the required residence period and other conditions, but permanent residency is not automatic. See the DNM's [permanent residency guidance](https://www.argentina.gob.ar/node/41181).

**If you are drawn to the idea of living here, the answer is yes: it is worth exploring seriously.** The numbers show that seeking Argentine residency is not an unusual idea for Americans, even if they cannot tell us how many ultimately settle here for good. The sensible next step is not to assume a move will be easy. It is to find out what kind of life you want here and which immigration route actually fits your circumstances.
