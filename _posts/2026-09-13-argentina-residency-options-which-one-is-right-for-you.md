# Argentina residency options: Which one is right for you?

**Short answer: Argentina has three main immigration categories: temporary residence, permanent residence and transitory residence. The right one depends mostly on what brings you to Argentina and how long you plan to stay.**
If you want to move here, get a DNI [National Identity Document] and establish yourself in Argentina, you will most likely be looking at **temporary residence**.
If you are coming for a shorter period, perhaps to study Spanish, take a tango course or work remotely for a few months, **transitory residence** may be enough.
And if you have already spent several years as a temporary resident, or fall within one of the specific categories established by law, **permanent residence** may be available.
Argentina formally recognizes these three categories. Temporary and permanent residents can obtain a DNI [National Identity Document]. **Transitory residents cannot.**

## Which one sounds like you?

You are probably looking for **temporary residence** if you want to make Argentina your home, obtain a DNI [National Identity Document] and stay for the medium or long term.
You may be looking for **transitory residence** if you want to spend a limited period here for a specific reason but do not need a DNI.
And you may be looking for **permanent residence** if you have already accumulated the required period as a temporary resident or qualify under one of the limited direct grounds.
For most people moving to Argentina for the first time, temporary residence is where the conversation starts.

## First, what is a precaria?

You will hear this word almost immediately if you start dealing with Argentine immigration.
A *residencia precaria* [provisional residence permit] **is not a fourth residency category**. It is a provisional authorization that Migraciones [Argentina's National Immigration Directorate] may issue while it decides your actual residency application.
It allows you to remain legally in Argentina while your file is pending, but it is not the temporary or permanent residence you applied for.
Under the rules currently in force, time held only under a *precaria* [provisional residence permit] does not count toward the period required for permanent residence.

## Temporary residence

Temporary residence is the main route for foreigners who want to actually establish themselves in Argentina.
It is granted because you meet a particular *criterio de radicación* [ground for residency]. Depending on that ground, temporary residence can be granted for different periods and renewed.
It also allows you to obtain a temporary DNI [National Identity Document]. Argentine law provides for temporary residence of up to three years depending on the category.
These are the grounds most people moving voluntarily to Argentina should look at first.

### MERCOSUR residence

If you are a national of a qualifying MERCOSUR [Southern Common Market] or associated country, your nationality itself can give you access to temporary residence.
This is one of the simplest routes because you do not need to create another reason for living here through employment, investment or study.
For someone who already qualifies through nationality, this is usually the first route worth considering.

### Rentista residence

For non-MERCOSUR nationals, *rentista* [passive-income] residence is **one of the most interesting options Argentina offers**.
It is available to people who can support themselves with income generated by assets they own.
Migraciones [Argentina's National Immigration Directorate] expressly recognizes income arising from assets such as **financial instruments, real estate and company participations**, and it may consider other types of assets as well. What does not fit this category is compensation generated by your personal work. A salary, freelance fee or consulting payment does not become passive income simply because it comes from abroad.
The current requirement is income equivalent to at least **five Salarios Mínimos, Vitales y Móviles [Minimum, Vital and Mobile Wages] per applicant**.
As of **September 2026**, one minimum wage is **ARS 383,800**, which means the current *rentista* [passive-income] threshold is:
**ARS 1,919,000 per month, per applicant.**
From **October 1, 2026**, the minimum wage increases to ARS 391,200, bringing the five-wage threshold to **ARS 1,956,000 per month**.
The amount changes whenever Argentina adjusts the minimum wage, so it should always be checked at the time of filing.
What makes *rentista* [passive-income] residence particularly interesting is that passive income can look very different from one person to another.
One applicant may own rental property. Another may have an investment portfolio. Someone else may receive income connected to an ownership interest in a company.
There is no single *rentista* profile.
The real question is: **what produces your income, and can we document it properly?**
For financially independent people, investors and property owners, this is often one of the first categories we look at.

### Pensionado residence

If you receive a regular pension or retirement benefit from abroad, the *pensionado* [pensioner] category may give you a route to temporary residence.
The pension needs to provide regular and permanent income. The current minimum is also equivalent to five Salarios Mínimos, Vitales y Móviles [Minimum, Vital and Mobile Wages], which as of September 2026 means **ARS 1,919,000 per month**.
Argentina is also genuinely worth considering as a place to retire.
Buenos Aires offers the culture, restaurants and services of a large capital, but if your idea of retirement involves mountains, lakes and a quieter pace of life, **Patagonia is without question one of the country's great options**.
Bariloche, San Martín de los Andes and Villa La Angostura offer a completely different lifestyle from Buenos Aires. For someone already receiving a pension abroad, Argentina can be both an immigration option and a compelling place to retire.

### Family reunification

A close family relationship can also provide a ground for temporary residence.
Under the current rules, this includes certain spouses, parents and children of Argentine citizens, as well as qualifying family members of temporary or permanent residents.
A registered *unión convivencial* [registered cohabitation union] can also be relevant in partner cases.
If your partner is Argentine, however, there is an important difference between being someone's boyfriend or girlfriend, living together and having a legally recognized relationship.

### Worker residence

If you are hired under an Argentine employment relationship, you may qualify as a migrant worker.
This generally means there is an Argentine employer supporting the application, together with the employment and company documentation required by Migraciones [Argentina's National Immigration Directorate].
This is not the same thing as working remotely.
If you live in Buenos Aires but receive your salary from a company in New York, London or Berlin, that does not automatically give you an Argentine worker-residency ground.

### Student residence

If you enroll as a regular student in an officially recognized Argentine secondary, tertiary, university or specialized program, you may qualify for temporary student residence.
The institution and program matter.
Being enrolled in something you personally consider a "course" does not necessarily mean that it supports temporary student residence.
This distinction becomes particularly useful for people who simply want to spend some time in Buenos Aires studying Spanish, tango or another non-degree subject. For them, **transitory residence may make much more sense**.

### Investor residence

Argentina also has an investor ground for temporary residence.
In practice, **this is not a route immigration lawyers usually recommend as a first option**. It is not commonly used, and the requirements and assessment of the investment make it considerably less straightforward than options such as MERCOSUR, *rentista* [passive-income], family or student residence.
That does not mean it is impossible to obtain.
For the right case, particularly where there is a genuine productive, commercial or service investment in Argentina, it remains a legal residency option.
What it is not is a simple "buy an apartment and get residency" program.
If someone qualifies under another clearer residency ground as well, it generally makes sense to compare the alternatives before choosing the investor route.

### Other temporary residence grounds

Argentine immigration law also provides for more specific situations, including **scientists and specialized personnel, corporate transfers, academics, professional athletes, artists, religious workers, people receiving certain medical treatment, refugees and asylees, humanitarian cases and other special circumstances recognized by Migraciones [Argentina's National Immigration Directorate].**
They exist, but for most people choosing to move to Argentina, the categories worth checking first are **MERCOSUR, rentista, pensionado, family, work, study and investment**.

## Permanent residence

Permanent residence is Argentina's long-term immigration status.
It does not have a fixed expiration date and allows you to obtain a permanent DNI [National Identity Document].
For most foreigners, permanent residence is something you **reach after holding temporary residence**, rather than the status you receive when you first move here.
Under the standard time-based rules, MERCOSUR nationals can generally apply after **two years of qualifying temporary residence**, while non-MERCOSUR nationals generally need **three years**.
Physical presence in Argentina also matters when qualifying through time as a temporary resident.
There are also limited situations in which permanent residence can be obtained through another direct legal ground.
So if Argentina is part of your long-term plan, the temporary residence you choose today can matter later.

## Transitory residence

This category is much more useful than many foreigners realize.
You may want to live legally in Argentina for a defined period without actually becoming a temporary resident.
Maybe you want one year in Buenos Aires to learn Spanish. Maybe you want to study tango seriously. Maybe you want to work remotely from Argentina for several months and then move on.
That is where **transitory residence** can make sense.
Transitory residence is intended for shorter stays connected to specific activities. Depending on the subcategory, it can be granted for up to one year.
And there is one important difference:
**Transitory residents cannot obtain a DNI [National Identity Document].**

### What if you just want to study Spanish or tango?

Argentina has a transitory student category for qualifying educational activities, including certain courses outside the official education system.
So if your plan is:
*"I want to spend a year in Buenos Aires, learn Spanish, take tango classes and be legally in Argentina,"*
you may not need to enroll in a university simply to create an immigration route.
A qualifying transitory student status may be enough.
The institution and the program still matter. Simply paying for tango classes or joining any language school does not automatically create an immigration ground.
And you will not receive a DNI [National Identity Document].
For someone who wants one interesting year in Argentina, that may be completely fine.
For someone who wants to make Argentina home, it probably is not.

### Digital nomads

Argentina's digital nomad status is also transitory.
It is aimed at eligible foreigners who work remotely for individuals or companies located outside Argentina.
It can be useful if Argentina is one part of a broader remote-working lifestyle.
It is much less useful if your actual goal is to obtain a DNI [National Identity Document], establish yourself here and eventually move toward permanent residence.

## Temporary, permanent or transitory?

Here is the simplest way to compare them:

| <br>                                    | **Temporary residence**                             | **Permanent residence**                              | **Transitory residence**                                   |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Best for**                            | People moving to Argentina                          | Long-term residents                                  | Shorter, specific stays                                    |
| **DNI [National Identity Document]**    | Yes, temporary DNI                                  | Yes, permanent DNI                                   | **No**                                                     |
| **Duration**                            | Up to 3 years depending on the ground               | No fixed expiration                                  | Up to 1 year depending on the category                     |
| **Typical examples**                    | MERCOSUR, rentista, pensionado, family, work, study | Time-based qualification and specific direct grounds | Short courses, digital nomads and other limited activities |
| **Part of a long-term residency plan?** | Yes                                                 | Yes                                                  | Usually no                                                 |

Migraciones [Argentina's National Immigration Directorate] expressly distinguishes between these three categories and confirms that transitory residence does not allow the holder to obtain a DNI [National Identity Document].

## Requirements

The exact requirements depend on the category.
In most cases, Migraciones [Argentina's National Immigration Directorate] will want to see your identity, legal entry into Argentina, domicile, criminal-record documentation when applicable, and evidence supporting the particular residency ground you chose.
That last part is what changes.
A *rentista* [passive-income applicant] proves income from assets. A *pensionado* [pensioner] proves a pension. A worker proves an Argentine employment relationship. A student proves qualifying enrollment. A family applicant proves the family relationship.

## Documents and apostilles

One practical piece of advice: **check your documents before you move**.
Foreign criminal records, civil-status documents and other public documents often need to be apostilled or otherwise legalized before they can be used in Argentina. Documents in another language may also need an official translation.
At **Life in Argentina**, this is one of the first things we review: what you should obtain before traveling, what needs an apostille and what can be handled once you arrive.
It is much easier to prepare the right document before your flight than to discover later that you need another certificate from home.

## The process

Most standard residence applications made from inside Argentina begin through RaDEX [Argentina's online residency system].
The general idea is straightforward: identify the right residency ground, prepare the documents, file the application, pay the government fees and follow the instructions issued by Migraciones [Argentina's National Immigration Directorate]. The official *rentista* process, for example, begins through RaDEX and requires the applicant to upload the documentation corresponding to the chosen residence.
The reality can be more tedious.
**Migraciones [Argentina's National Immigration Directorate] is bureaucratic, and small problems can slow a file down.**
A missing apostille, inconsistent information or a request for another document can turn a straightforward application into several rounds of follow-up.
The best way to make the process easier is to start with the right residency ground and a properly prepared file.

## How long does it take?

There is no single processing time for Argentine residency.
A straightforward MERCOSUR application is very different from a *rentista* [passive-income] case involving several assets abroad or a family case requiring additional verification.
The timeline depends heavily on the category and on whether your documentation is ready when you start.
If you already know you are moving to Argentina, preparing the immigration side before your arrival usually saves time later.

## Common problems

The same issues come up again and again:

- choosing the wrong residency ground;
- treating salary or freelance income as *rentista* [passive-income] income;
- arriving with documents that were never apostilled;
- enrolling in a course without checking whether it supports the immigration status you want; and
- assuming that a *precaria* [provisional residence permit] means the final residence has already been approved.

Most of these problems can be fixed.
It is simply easier not to create them in the first place.

## FAQ

### What are the three residency categories in Argentina?

Argentina recognizes **temporary residence, permanent residence and transitory residence**.
A *precaria* [provisional residence permit] is not a fourth category.

### Which types of residence give me a DNI?

**Temporary and permanent residence.**
Transitory residence does not.

### What is the easiest residency in Argentina?

It depends on what you already qualify for.
If you are a qualifying MERCOSUR national, nationality is usually the obvious place to start.
If you are a non-MERCOSUR national with passive income, *rentista* [passive-income] residence may be particularly interesting.
If you have an Argentine spouse or qualifying family relationship, family reunification may make more sense.
The simplest residency is often the one based on something that is already true about your life.

### How much income do I need for rentista residence?

As of **September 2026**, the requirement is the equivalent of five Salarios Mínimos, Vitales y Móviles [Minimum, Vital and Mobile Wages].
That currently equals **ARS 1,919,000 per month, per applicant**.
From October 1, 2026, the threshold increases to **ARS 1,956,000 per month**.
Because the minimum wage changes, always check the current figure before applying.

### I receive passive income. Can I qualify as a rentista?

Potentially.
Migraciones [Argentina's National Immigration Directorate] currently recognizes income generated by assets such as real estate, financial instruments and company participations, and may consider other qualifying assets as well.
How the income is generated and how you prove it are both important.

### I work remotely for a foreign company. Am I a rentista?

Not because of your salary.
*Rentista* [passive-income] income comes from assets rather than payment for your personal work.
If you work remotely for foreign clients or a foreign employer, Argentina's digital nomad category may be relevant, although it is a **transitory status**, not temporary residence.

### Can I get residency by buying property in Argentina?

Not simply by buying property.
Argentina does not currently have a standard real-estate "golden visa."
Property may still be relevant in other ways. For example, qualifying rental income generated by an asset you own may potentially form part of a *rentista* [passive-income] application.

### Can I stay in Argentina for a year just to study Spanish?

Potentially, yes.
A qualifying educational program may fit Argentina's transitory student framework.
The institution and course need to satisfy the applicable immigration requirements, and the result is transitory rather than temporary residence.

### What about tango?

Potentially the same route.
A qualifying tango program may fit within the transitory student framework, depending on the institution and program.
Simply paying for weekly tango classes does not create immigration status.

### Will I get a DNI with transitory student residence?

No.
**Transitory residents cannot obtain a DNI [National Identity Document].**

### How do I eventually become a permanent resident?

For many foreigners, permanent residence comes after a qualifying period as a temporary resident.
The exact period and requirements depend on your nationality and immigration history, so the route should be reviewed before assuming that simply holding a temporary card for a certain number of years will be enough.

You may qualify for more than one.
Someone researching work residence may already qualify through MERCOSUR. Someone working remotely may also own assets that make *rentista* [passive-income] residence possible. Someone planning to enroll in a full degree may discover that a transitory student status is enough for the year they actually want to spend here.
