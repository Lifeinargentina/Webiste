# Can I Get Residency in Argentina by Renting Out a Property I Own?

**How rental income from a property abroad can help you qualify for Argentina's Rentista residency, what documents you need, and how to prepare your application.**

Owning a property in your home country could open the door to living legally in Argentina. If you rent out an apartment, house, or other property and receive a regular monthly income, you may already have one of the most important requirements for obtaining temporary residency in Argentina.

At Life in Argentina, this is one of the residency options we most frequently recommend to clients who want to relocate without depending on an Argentine employer, enrolling in a university, or making a substantial new investment. Many people come to us believing that obtaining residency requires finding a job in Argentina or purchasing property locally. In reality, the property they already own abroad may be enough to establish the financial basis for an application.

Argentina recognizes a specific immigration category known as *residencia temporaria como rentista*, or temporary residency based on income from personal assets. Rental income is particularly interesting because it can be supported by documents that immigration authorities can examine: property ownership records, lease agreements, bank statements, and evidence of recurring payments.

Of course, owning a property does not automatically qualify someone for residency. The income must meet certain legal requirements, and the applicant must be able to demonstrate where the money comes from, how regularly it is received, and how it can be brought into Argentina.

In this article, we explain how the process works, why rental income can be a strong basis for a Rentista application, and what we look for when evaluating a client's eligibility.

## 1. Can rental income qualify you for residency in Argentina?

Yes. Rental income from a property you own abroad can qualify as a source of income for Argentina's Rentista temporary residency category, provided that the income and the application meet the applicable legal requirements.

Under Article 23(b) of Argentina's Immigration Law No. 25,871, the Rentista category is intended for foreign nationals who can support themselves through their own resources and income derived from sources outside Argentina.

The Dirección Nacional de Migraciones (DNM), Argentina's national immigration authority, expressly recognizes income generated by assets belonging to the applicant, including real estate, financial instruments, and business interests.

This means that someone who owns an apartment in New York, a house in Florida, a property in London, or a rental unit in another country may be eligible to apply for temporary residency based on the income generated by that property.

The important distinction is that **the property itself is not what qualifies you. It is the income generated by the property, together with the evidence supporting that income.**

For example, imagine that you own an apartment in your home country and rent it to a tenant under a formal lease agreement. Every month, the tenant transfers the rent into your bank account. You can provide documents proving that you own the apartment, that the lease exists, and that you regularly receive the rental payments.

That is the kind of financial arrangement that can provide a strong foundation for a Rentista application.

## 2. Why do we frequently recommend rental income as a residency option?

At Life in Argentina, we work with foreign nationals who want to make Argentina their home, whether they are moving permanently, retiring, joining a partner, or simply looking for a different way of life.

One of the first things we examine is whether the person already has assets or income abroad that could support a residency application.

Rental income is one of our preferred options because it often provides a relatively straightforward way to demonstrate the three elements that immigration authorities need to understand: **ownership of the asset, the legal origin of the income, and its continuity over time.**

Unlike income from certain financial investments, which may fluctuate from month to month, rental income under a properly documented lease can be more predictable. A written contract establishes the relationship between the landlord and the tenant, identifies the property, and specifies the amount and frequency of payments.

From a documentary perspective, this can make a rental-income application easier to explain and support.

It is also an option that fits naturally with the plans of many of our clients. Someone relocating to Argentina may prefer to keep their property abroad rather than sell it immediately. Renting out that property can generate income while preserving an asset in their home country.

We consider this a particularly attractive route because it is expressly contemplated by the immigration framework and can be supported by familiar, verifiable financial documents.

That does not mean the Dirección Nacional de Migraciones automatically prefers or approves applications based on rental income. Each application is reviewed individually. However, a properly structured rental arrangement can offer a clear evidentiary basis for demonstrating eligibility.

## 3. How much rental income do I need for a Rentista visa in Argentina?

Argentina's Rentista residency requirements establish a minimum income equivalent to **five times the Argentine minimum monthly wage**, known as the *Salario Mínimo, Vital y Móvil* (SMVM).

This requirement is established by DNM Disposition No. 1732/2023, as amended by Disposition No. 3446/2023.

Because Argentina's minimum wage changes periodically, the amount required in Argentine pesos can also change. For this reason, we generally advise clients against relying on a fixed amount in US dollars found in older articles, online forums, or immigration discussions.

A rental arrangement that qualifies today may need to be reassessed if the minimum income threshold changes before the application is submitted.

For example, if you receive rent in US dollars, euros, or British pounds, the relevant question is whether that income satisfies the applicable threshold when assessed under the immigration authority's requirements.

It is also important to consider whether the rental income is stable, properly documented, and sufficient to support the proposed residency application.

At Life in Argentina, we review these factors before recommending that a client proceed. Our goal is to identify potential problems before the application reaches the immigration authorities, rather than discover them after documents have been submitted.

## 4. Does the rental property have to be in my name?

This is one of the most important questions we receive, particularly from couples and families planning to relocate together.

The Rentista category requires the applicant to demonstrate that the income originates from assets incorporated into their own patrimony. In practical terms, ownership matters.

If you own the property individually, the documentary relationship between the asset and the income is generally easier to establish.

If the property is jointly owned with your spouse, partner, or another person, the analysis becomes more complicated. We need to determine who legally owns the property, who is entitled to receive the rent, and how the income is distributed.

For example, a married couple may own a property together while the rental payments are deposited into only one spouse's bank account. That arrangement does not necessarily prevent an application, but it requires a careful review of the ownership documents, applicable property law, and immigration requirements.

We also encounter situations where a client receives rental income from a property owned by a parent or another family member. In those cases, simply receiving money into a bank account does not establish that the applicant owns the income-producing asset.

**Our recommendation is to establish the legal relationship between the applicant, the property, and the rental income before preparing the application.**

This is especially important when more than one family member intends to obtain residency.

## 5. What if I own a property but haven't rented it out yet?

Owning a valuable property does not necessarily mean you qualify for Rentista residency.

A person may own a house worth several hundred thousand dollars but receive no income from it. Although that property represents substantial personal wealth, the Rentista category is primarily concerned with income generated by qualifying assets.

If the property is currently vacant, one possibility is to rent it out before applying for residency.

However, we would need to evaluate whether the proposed rental arrangement can satisfy the applicable requirements concerning the amount, stability, and documentation of the income.

A signed lease agreement may help demonstrate a future entitlement to rental payments, but it should not be assumed that a newly executed contract, without evidence of actual payments, will automatically satisfy the immigration authorities.

This is why we encourage clients who are planning to relocate in the coming months to contact us before making significant changes to their financial arrangements.

In some cases, preparing the rental arrangement in advance can make the eventual application considerably more straightforward.

## 6. What documents do I need to prove my rental income?

The exact documentation depends on the country where the property is located, the applicant's ownership structure, and the circumstances of the rental arrangement.

Nevertheless, when preparing a Rentista application based on property income, we generally begin by examining several documents.

**Proof of property ownership.** This may include a title deed, an official land registry certificate, or another legally recognized document establishing that the applicant owns the property.

**A valid rental agreement.** The lease should identify the property, the landlord and tenant, the agreed rental amount, the payment frequency, and the duration of the arrangement.

**Evidence of rental payments.** Bank statements or other financial records can help establish that the income is actually being received and that it corresponds to the rental agreement.

**Evidence of the origin and movement of funds.** Immigration authorities need to understand where the income comes from and how the funds enter Argentina through authorized financial channels.

**Personal immigration documents.** Applicants must also satisfy the general residency requirements, which can include a valid passport, criminal background certificates, proof of address in Argentina, and evidence of lawful entry.

Foreign documents may require an apostille or other applicable legalization, together with a certified translation into Spanish when necessary.

Not every application requires precisely the same supporting documents. The Dirección Nacional de Migraciones may request additional evidence depending on the circumstances.

At Life in Argentina, we focus on preparing a coherent documentary record in which the ownership documents, lease agreement, financial statements, and immigration application tell the same story.

A document may be legally valid in its country of origin and still be insufficient to prove a particular fact required by Argentina's immigration authorities.

That distinction is one of the reasons why preparing the application correctly matters.

## 7. Do I need to transfer my rental income to Argentina?

Yes, the movement of funds is an important part of the Rentista requirements.

Argentina's immigration regulations require applicants to demonstrate the origin of their funds and their entry into the country through banking or financial institutions authorized by the Banco Central de la República Argentina.

This does not mean that an applicant should immediately transfer all their savings or rental income into Argentina before obtaining legal advice.

Rather, the applicant needs to understand how the income will be received, transferred, and documented in a manner consistent with the applicable immigration and financial rules.

For example, a person may receive rent in a bank account in the United States and subsequently transfer funds to Argentina through an authorized institution.

The documentation should allow the authorities to understand the connection between the rental income received abroad and the funds brought into Argentina.

We regularly explain to clients that proving ownership of a property and proving the movement of rental income are two different parts of the application.

Both deserve attention.

## 8. Can my spouse or partner apply using the same rental income?

Possibly, but this is an area where individual circumstances matter considerably.

Couples often contact us because one person owns a property that generates enough income to support both of them. They naturally assume that the same rental income can be used to obtain residency for the entire household.

However, Argentina's Rentista regulations contain specific income requirements for applicants, and the authorities may examine the financial position of each person seeking residency under that category.

A jointly owned property, a marriage, or a shared bank account does not automatically resolve those requirements.

Depending on the couple's circumstances, it may be appropriate to examine whether both individuals can independently establish eligibility or whether another immigration category or family-based route is available.

At Life in Argentina, we evaluate the family's situation as a whole rather than assuming that the same strategy will necessarily work for every member.

## 9. How long does Rentista residency last?

Rentista is a temporary residency category.

Argentina's Immigration Law allows this category to be granted for up to three years, subject to the applicable administrative rules. The DNM's current public guidance for ordinary Rentista temporary residency applications describes a one-year permit that may be renewed.

The duration actually granted therefore depends on the applicable procedure and the immigration authority's decision.

During the renewal process, applicants may need to demonstrate that they continue to meet the conditions under which their residency was originally granted.

For someone relying on rental income, this makes continuity particularly important.

If a tenant moves out, the lease expires, or the property is sold, those changes may affect the financial circumstances supporting the residency.

They do not necessarily mean that the person immediately loses their immigration status, but they should be assessed before the next application or renewal.

## 10. Can Rentista residency lead to permanent residency in Argentina?

Temporary Rentista residency can form part of a longer-term immigration strategy for someone who intends to settle in Argentina.

However, temporary residency and permanent residency are different legal statuses, and holding a Rentista permit does not automatically guarantee that permanent residency will be granted.

Eligibility depends on the applicable immigration rules, the applicant's nationality, the period of qualifying legal residence, physical presence requirements, and other relevant circumstances.

For clients who intend to make Argentina their long-term home, we consider these questions from the beginning.

It is important to understand not only how to obtain the first residency permit, but also how to maintain lawful status and what options may become available in the future.

## 11. What about taxes on my rental property abroad?

Immigration residency and tax residency are not the same thing.

A foreign national may qualify for Rentista immigration status based on income from a property abroad, but that does not by itself determine how the income will be taxed.

Argentina has its own rules concerning tax residence, foreign-source income, and the reporting of assets held outside the country.

The consequences depend on the person's circumstances, the country where the property is located, and the applicable domestic laws and international tax agreements.

This is particularly relevant for people moving from the United States, where citizenship-based federal taxation can continue to apply even after relocating abroad.

At Life in Argentina, we work with specialized accountants when clients need to evaluate the tax consequences of relocating, retaining property abroad, or receiving foreign rental income while living in Argentina.

We believe these questions should be addressed before the move, not after the person has already established their life in the country.
